## Usage

Choose type roles before individual treatments. Body size, measure, and leading establish the reading conditions from which text columns can be built. A text cut serves sustained reading; a display cut can serve large, brief messages when the family provides both.

Display copy benefits from a small hierarchy. Editorial, interface, legal, data, and complex navigation work can need additional recurring functional roles. Script-specific conventions take precedence over English word counts, tracking, capitalization, alignment, and line-break defaults.

## Rules

- Assign every size and weight a named role; repeat the same combined style for the same content role. Start with one family; use more than two only for a clear conceptual or functional reason.
- Use true designed bold, italic, and other faces when available. Prefer tabular numerals in aligned numerical data and proportional numerals in prose.
- For display compositions, use three primary size levels by default: primary, supporting, and detail. Two genuine information levels need only two sizes. Additional display levels need a functional reason; larger systems may have additional named, recurring utility sizes.
- Sizes representing different hierarchy levels differ by at least 3 px in digital work or 3 pt in print. Prefer adjacent primary levels at least 1.2 times apart and display headline/body levels at least 1.35 times apart. Consolidate levels that remain visually interchangeable at final size.
- Use no more than three weights per composition by default, and only two when sufficient. Additional semantic weights in complex systems need a functional role; remove visually interchangeable weights.
- Use size for primary information levels and weight, underline, color, spacing, or position for local emphasis. Prefer one emphasis device at a time, with consistent underline weight and offset that avoids descender collisions.
- In interfaces, reserve underlines primarily for links, interactive text, or explicitly defined states; in noninteractive editorial and display work they may provide deliberate emphasis.
- In display work, prefer headlines of about 3–10 words and 1–3 lines; more than 12 words needs an editorial, legal, localization, or conceptual reason. Prefer short-form paragraphs of about 12–16 words and 1–3 lines; split or edit those exceeding 24 words.
- Keep editorial and interface paragraphs focused on one idea; divide complex instructions into meaningful sections, steps, or lists rather than applying display paragraph limits.
- For standard body copy use approximately 45–75 characters per line, including spaces and punctuation; target 60–66 for sustained reading where possible. Short display copy may use 20–55 and narrow mobile columns 35–55. Code, tables, mathematics, captions, navigation, expressive type, and different writing systems may need other measures.
- Adjust width, column count, copy, or type size when measure falls outside its intended range; test the longest expected headline, paragraph, label, and legal passage.
- Begin screen body leading around 1.3–1.6 times type size, print body around 1.2–1.45, and display around 0.9–1.15. Adjust for face, x-height, weight, language, measure, and reproduction; wider lines usually need more leading. Repeated body roles keep consistent leading.
- Keep visual interline space greater than interletter space; use body leading as the basis of vertical rhythm.
- Use left-aligned ragged-right body copy for left-to-right languages. Right alignment needs a right-hand axis or right-to-left language. Centering is for short composed display text, preferably no more than three lines.
- Use full justification only in editorial work with active control of measure, word spacing, hyphenation, and copy fitting. Use paragraph spacing or first-line indentation rather than both; keep a heading closer to its following content than to preceding content.
- Shape headline breaks by meaning and natural phrasing. Keep rags natural, avoid isolated paragraph starts or ends at column/page edges, and keep at least two body lines with a heading when content continues elsewhere.
- Use selective hyphenation, no more than two consecutive hyphenated lines; avoid names, short words, URLs, and prominent display copy. Avoid one-word final lines in prominent paragraphs or headlines unless deliberate emphasis serves the message.
- Begin with native type spacing and keep body tracking near default. Modest positive tracking may aid short all-caps labels; manually kern only large display type with visible spacing problems.
- Recompose headlines and line endings for each language while preserving primary hierarchy; follow its writing and number conventions.

## Never

- Never artificially embolden, italicize, stretch, condense, skew, or distort type — instead use a designed face or appropriate family.
- Never introduce a size or weight merely for variety, or a new size for a single emphasized word — instead use meaningful styles and local emphasis within the weight budget.
- Never use every size at every weight — instead keep a limited set of meaningful combined styles.
- Never shrink text solely to fit excess copy or an arbitrary box, or widen text to occupy empty space — instead edit or recompose for the intended measure.
- Never let standard paragraphs regularly exceed 80 characters per line or collapse into repeated one- or two-word lines — instead adjust the measure.
- Never center sustained body copy or accept rivers and holes in justification — instead use a reading alignment with controlled spacing.
- Never insert manual breaks in normal body paragraphs — instead let the text reflow.
- Never tightly track small body copy, overtrack lowercase text, or set long paragraphs in capitals — instead preserve readable native rhythm.
- Never apply typographic effects by default or use them to repair weak hierarchy — instead establish clear roles; use effects only for a concept, brand treatment, affordance, or needed separation.
- Never fill type with images or patterns without a deliberate concept and sufficient legibility — instead use a readable treatment.
